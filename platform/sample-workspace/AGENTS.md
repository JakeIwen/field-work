# Job Search Writing Guidelines

When drafting or revising job-search materials for an applicant, especially software-engineering cover letters, resumes, recruiter messages, LinkedIn content, and application answers, follow these principles.

## General style

Write like an experienced software professional, not like a polished marketing document or generic AI-generated application.

* Prefer straightforward, natural professional prose.
* Avoid em dashes as a default punctuation device.
* Avoid formulaic transitions, symmetrical sentence structures, inflated phrasing, obvious LLM-style polish, and short declarative sentences that narrate the document's own structure.
* Do not use generic enthusiasm such as “I am thrilled,” “I am passionate,” or “I am excited to bring my skills...” unless there is a specific reason that wording is justified.
* Avoid empty claims such as “hardworking,” “team player,” “fast learner,” or “results-driven.” Demonstrate those qualities through evidence instead.
* Keep wording precise, grounded, and technically credible.
* Preserve the applicant’s actual voice when revising material. Do not make it sound more corporate merely for the sake of sounding professional.

### Natural company-facing prose

Apply these rules to cover letters, recruiter messages, application answers, and other employer-facing prose alongside the general style guidance above. Keep resume skills and technical evidence where they help the reader.

* **Open with the strongest relevant connection.** Lead with an experience, engineering problem, or specific reason for interest that helps explain this application, weighing relevance, professional stakes, recency, and specificity. Professional evidence is the usual default; a personal project can lead when it supplies the clearest evidence for a central need. Do not open with a generic fit announcement or a restatement of the role title, and avoid formulaic openings that merely announce that a role “appeals to me,” “overlaps with my experience,” or is “a strong fit.”
* **Let evidence carry the claim.** After a concrete example, add interpretation only when it gives the reader useful context or explains a specific connection to the role. Cut generic lessons such as “Those responsibilities have shaped how I weigh feature delivery against the maintenance a product needs.” Do not give every example a concluding statement about what it demonstrates. A short phrase may name the working habit a concrete example shows (communication, review style, client collaboration) when it states the audience and the form, such as a documented review procedure or a concrete plan written for its intended reader. That is how collaboration and communication enter a letter; the applicant's evidence should establish those examples and their limits. Invented anecdotes, formal mentoring or management claims, and adjectives with no attached example stay out.
* **Start paragraphs with content, not labels.** Do not open a paragraph with a short abstract sentence that announces what the paragraph will do or vouches for its own content, such as “The gaps are concrete.”, “Two gaps are worth naming:”, or “Release safety was something I built rather than assumed.” Begin with the fact or the specific connection itself; the reader should not need the letter's structure explained. A label sentence belongs only where it is the direct answer to a question that asked for exactly that, such as an application question about missing experience.
* **Make the evidence interpretable.** Give each substantive paragraph a central point, with enough concrete evidence to explain what the applicant did, what constraint mattered, and why it is relevant. Prefer a few developed examples over inventories of accomplishments or technologies. Use the posting's accurate terminology where it makes a supported match easier to recognize; do not force exact phrases, comprehensive coverage, or a repetition quota. Keep causal explanations, such as how a stale worker is prevented from overwriting a replacement result.
* **Prefer relevant past behavior to broad promises.** Replace statements such as “I would bring that same attention to your team” with supported details about what the applicant did, or omit the promise when the example already makes the point. Do not invent experiences, motivations, anecdotes, or outcomes to make the prose feel personal.
* **Keep technology lists purposeful.** In prose, mention technologies when they explain a specific accomplishment, requirement match, or experience gap. Leave general stack inventories to the resume instead of repeating them in each letter or answer.
* **Vary sentence openings.** First-person prose slides into starting every sentence with "I" or a contraction of it (I've, I'd, I'll, I'm). Never start two consecutive sentences that way, in any employer-facing prose. In a cover letter or a multi-paragraph answer, keep such sentences to about one in three at most; a short direct answer may open with "I" when the question asks about the applicant. Start the other sentences with the problem, the system, the constraint or the client, then the action: "The import job had to reject incomplete files before publishing, so..." or "The query needed a stable ordering for pagination while...". Do not dodge "I" with passive voice, "My" openings or inverted word order; rewrite the sentence around its subject.
* **Handle gaps selectively and constructively.** Mention a skill shortcoming or mismatch when it prevents a misleading impression or clarifies an important transition, but do not leave the discrepancy hanging as a performance of honesty. In a cover letter, connect it to a concrete reason the transition is credible: relevant transferable experience, demonstrated ability to learn unfamiliar tools, or a specific willingness to learn grounded in the role. The point must help the employer assess how the applicant could do the work; an unrelated accomplishment or a generic “fast learner” claim does not complete it. Use only documented examples of learning or transferable work, selectively rather than as a mandatory anecdote or stock paragraph. Keep adjacent experience distinct from direct experience, and do not invent enthusiasm, a learning plan, a ramp-up speed, or expertise to compensate. Omit an unnecessary gap rather than volunteering a bare deficit; retain any disclosure needed for accuracy and explain its practical significance. Do not inventory missing nice-to-haves, force a gap paragraph, or impose a numerical cap. Read required/preferred and AND/OR conditions before calling anything a gap. Discuss a departure only when relevant and supported by applicant-approved facts. Keep the wording factual and free of blame; omit internal details that are unnecessary for the employer's assessment.
* **Describe responsibility in plain terms.** Explain what the applicant maintained, who depended on it, what constraint mattered, or what needed to keep working. Avoid repeatedly packaging experience as “long-term ownership,” “operational workflows,” or “technical tradeoffs.” These remain useful concepts, not stock phrases to insert into every paragraph.
* **Preserve ownership, status, and material limitations.** Choose verbs and short qualifiers that distinguish contributions, ownership, prototypes, completed implementations, and operated production systems. Remove internal audit and process language (“approved pending consolidation,” “deployment still planned”), but retain a qualification when the verb alone would leave a misleading impression. Distinguish personal projects from professional or customer use, and do not imply unaided implementation or adoption the evidence does not establish. Explain AI assistance when it is relevant to that distinction or to the question, consolidating the explanation where its scope remains clear.
* **Allow a short ending.** Do not repeat the ownership, collaboration, and company-fit argument in the closing. A brief, natural invitation to talk is enough when a closing is appropriate; direct application answers need no ceremonial closing.
* **Edit for the reader, not a detector score.** AI-detector highlights can prompt an editorial review, but scores are not proof of authorship or a writing-quality target. Preserve accurate technical detail and natural wording even when flagged. Do not add errors, forced informality, arbitrary sentence variation, or awkward synonyms to chase a lower score.

For each sentence, ask: **Does it add a fact, a specific reason, or necessary context?** If it only announces fit, summarizes virtues, or repeats the paragraph's point, cut it. Preserve useful interpretation rather than turning the letter into a list of resume facts.

### Review triggers

These prompt a second look under the sentence test above. No trigger deletes a sentence on its own; a sentence that survives a company-name swap may still provide necessary evidence or interpretation.

* A passage carried over from the evidence dossier's answer starters or from an earlier application with light edits.
* A fit statement that would read the same with another employer's name substituted.
* Accumulating examples in one paragraph, or repeated “I also” transitions.
* Repeated hedges or disclosures.
* A skill gap or mismatch left without a useful connection to transferable experience, learning, or how I could do the work.
* A paragraph that opens with a label sentence announcing its purpose or asserting the quality of its own content.
* Vague scope language (“spent years,” “a small team,” “supported” for something the applicant owned) where the evidence is specific. Use “supported,” “contributed,” “maintained,” or “owned” according to the evidence, and prefer specific scope when it helps the reader.
* Two consecutive sentences starting with "I", or more than about one sentence in three starting that way in a letter or multi-paragraph answer; contractions of "I" count.
* Cuts made solely to satisfy a renderer rather than by editorial choice.

## Cover letters

Treat a cover letter as a short argument for fit, not as a traditional ceremonial business letter.

A strong cover letter should answer:

1. Why does this particular role make sense for the applicant?
2. Why does the applicant's background map especially well to what the employer needs?
3. What useful context about the applicant is not obvious from scanning the resume?

### Length and structure

* The roughly 250–400 word range, usually around 300–350, is guidance rather than a padding target.
* Keep it to one page.
* Prefer approximately 3–4 substantive paragraphs with a simple greeting and closing:

  * Opening that makes the strongest relevant connection.
  * Paragraph connecting the most important job requirements to relevant experience.
  * Paragraph explaining a meaningful differentiator, engineering approach, or broader context.
  * Short closing.
* Do not pad the letter to make it longer.
* Check the final source's length and the rendered PDF for complete text, readable spacing, and one-page fit. Resolve accidental layout inflation in the renderer before cutting valuable evidence; if a letter is still too long with correct, readable layout, edit according to evidence priorities.

### Personalization

Personalize the letter substantively, not cosmetically.

* Reference actual responsibilities, technologies, engineering problems, product characteristics, or organizational needs from the job listing.
* Explain why those aspects overlap with the applicant's experience.
* Do not merely repeat the company name, mission statement, or generic statements about innovation.
* Facts and effective wording may be reused. A passage borrowed from the evidence dossier or another application needs review for relevance, emphasis, sufficiency, and employer-facing register; rewrite it to serve the present argument, not merely to replace words with synonyms.
* Spend more personalization effort on unusually strong-fit or especially desirable roles.

### Relationship to the resume

Do not convert resume bullets into paragraphs.

The resume should contain evidence. The cover letter should help the reader interpret that evidence.

For example, rather than repeating several implementation bullets, explain the broader pattern:

> An import pipeline had to tolerate incomplete partner files without replacing the last valid dataset. Explain the validation and publication boundary, not just the list of parser changes. This is a fictional illustration, not a claim about an applicant.

Use individual accomplishments as evidence for broader ideas such as:

* Long-term ownership of production systems.
* Technical judgment and tradeoff evaluation.
* Modernizing mature or legacy applications without disrupting users.
* Moving effectively across frontend, backend, APIs, databases, infrastructure, and integrations.
* Debugging problems across system boundaries.
* Building reusable infrastructure instead of repeatedly solving the same problem.
* Understanding the business or operational workflow behind the software.
* Maintaining and supporting systems after they ship.
* Working effectively on a small engineering team with significant individual responsibility.

Choose a high-level narrative only when the applicant's evidence supports it. Do not impose a stock identity or repeat an approved phrase in every application.

## Resume writing

Optimize for clarity, credibility, and meaningful engineering evidence rather than maximum apparent impressiveness. Two-page readability and evidence priorities govern layout decisions; exact preservation of an earlier version's spare space is a verification result, not an editorial requirement.

### Metrics and quantitative claims

Avoid metric theater and “precision without explanatory context.”

* Do not use an exact number merely because it sounds impressive.
* A metric should clarify the nature, scale, or significance of the engineering work.
* Avoid numbers that could imply a larger, more difficult, or materially different accomplishment than what actually happened.
* Prefer rounded scale descriptions when exact precision adds little:

  * “dozens of records” rather than a count that adds no useful context.
  * “several integrations” when exact precision is immaterial.
  * Rounded numbers must still be supported by the evidence; these are not applicant facts.
* Preserve exact figures when the exact value is meaningful and defensible.

When a dramatic result arose from a specific technical mechanism, explain the mechanism rather than making the raw ratio the headline.

Prefer:

> Added a composite index matching the query filters and ordering so the database could avoid a full-table sort. This is a fictional mechanism example, not an applicant accomplishment.

over:

> Made the query dramatically faster.

The quantitative result can still appear when it adds useful context, but the engineering decision should remain central.

### Bullet construction

Prefer bullets that communicate:

**problem/context → engineering action → meaningful result or significance**

Not every bullet needs all three explicitly, but the reader should understand why the work mattered.

Favor strong, accurate verbs such as:

* built
* designed
* implemented
* migrated
* maintained
* automated
* integrated
* diagnosed
* refactored
* modernized
* owned

Avoid repeatedly using vague verbs such as:

* helped
* worked on
* participated in
* leveraged
* utilized

Do not exaggerate ownership. Distinguish clearly between work the applicant owned, contributed to, inherited or maintained.

### Naming decisions

Use the applicant's approved terminology consistently across general masters, tailored copies and letters. Preserve official product names, exact encoding names and distinctions between similarly named technologies. Do not invent naming preferences or copy another applicant's project vocabulary. Keep sentence-specific approvals and supporting evidence with the applicant's source material rather than turning them into mandatory stock prose.

## Adapting the same experience across formats

Do not mechanically reuse resume wording.

Use different levels of abstraction depending on the medium:

* **Resume:** concise evidence and technical specifics.
* **Cover letter:** interpretation of that evidence and why it matches the role.
* **Recruiter message:** a few strongest points of fit.
* **Application question:** direct response using only the evidence relevant to the question. It may open with the summary sentence the question asks for; the same sentence would be a label in a letter.
* **Interview answer:** context, decision-making, tradeoffs, and outcome.
* **LinkedIn:** readable professional summary with somewhat less implementation detail.

Preserve the underlying facts across all formats, but rewrite them appropriately for the purpose.

## Technical relevance

When reading a software-engineering job listing, identify the handful of requirements that appear central rather than attempting to match every keyword.

Before drafting, identify the role's few consequential responsibilities and qualifications and map them to supported experience. Distinguish direct evidence, adjacent experience, and unknowns, and read required/preferred and AND/OR conditions accurately. Use this as a drafting check, not a checklist to reproduce in the letter. Prioritize the actual work and its consequences over a technology inventory or the posting's section order. Requirements for communication, collaboration, or mentoring map to the applicant's documented examples, including their stated limits.

Write that map down before drafting, organized by the posting's few consequential needs rather than the letter's paragraphs. For each need, record the strongest supported evidence, whether it is direct or adjacent, and any material limitation, or “none” where evidence is absent. After drafting, check whether useful supported evidence was omitted and whether the omission is deliberate. The map is working material, not a checklist to reproduce in the letter or a requirement to cover every posting item. Treat advice about which facts to emphasize, including any source-evidence framing cautions, as an audience-dependent default: when a posting specifically values a technology, platform, domain, or kind of work that general guidance de-emphasizes, bring the supported evidence forward in terms that reader will recognize. This does not relax limits on ownership, deployment status, scale, or other factual claims.

Prioritize meaningful overlap in the role's central responsibilities, technologies, operating constraints, collaboration requirements and ownership scope. Select examples from the applicant's evidence rather than importing a standard technology inventory.

Do not manufacture experience simply because a technology appears in the listing. Adjacent experience can be described as adjacent experience.

## Final quality check

Before returning job-search copy, check that:

* It sounds like a real software engineer could have written it.
* It contains no invented accomplishments, technologies, metrics, or responsibilities.
* Important claims can be supported by known resume/project evidence.
* It is specific enough that it does not read like a generic application template.
* It does not simply restate the resume.
* Each sentence adds a fact, a specific reason, or necessary context; generic fit statements, repeated lessons, unnecessary technology lists, and padded closings have been removed.
* Quantitative claims are contextualized and defensible.
* The level of formality matches a modern US software-company hiring process.
* Unnecessary AI-writing mannerisms have been removed.
* Verbs and qualifiers match the evidence for ownership and status (contribution, ownership, prototype, completed implementation, operated in production), and personal projects are not presented as professional or customer use.
* Reused passages were reviewed for relevance, emphasis, sufficiency, and register, and the review triggers were applied.
* For a letter, the rendered PDF was checked for complete text, readable spacing, and one-page fit.

## Job-search harvest completeness

For new imports, apply the applicant's explicitly saved exclusion criteria to the title and full posting, including optional or preferred requirements when the configured rule covers them. Preserve previously saved listings. Use the same captured criteria across careers scans, pulls and direct URL research; do not infer an exclusion list from another applicant's skillset.

When harvesting search results, keep a per-posting ledger that distinguishes discovery, date/title triage, source-backed review, deferral/exclusion, and an existing or newly saved Fieldwork record. Do not substitute a hand-picked shortlist for a requested complete harvest. Account for every result in the requested searches; record why irrelevant suggestions are excluded.

For Greenhouse, use the user's latest freshness window and original `first_published`, not `updated_at`. Inspect actual duties and remote eligibility; search labels alone are insufficient. Preserve previously saved openings when the window changes.

Check both on-screen notices and HTTP responses during pagination. An HTTP 429, API-limit notice, failed request, repeated cards, or scrolling with no new results is not proof of exhaustion. Retain the failed page/cursor, respect `Retry-After` or a conservative cooldown, and resume the same page without duplicate imports. Pace requests and never bypass limits. Claim complete coverage only with successful terminal pagination evidence for every requested query and no unaccounted relevant listings or pending approved imports. Report remaining gaps explicitly.
